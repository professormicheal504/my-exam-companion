JAMB Raises Minimum Score Benchmark — What Every 2026 Candidate Must Know

Sarah Johnson
Aug 4, 2026 4 min read
Photo via Unsplash. Students preparing for the upcoming exams.

The Joint Admissions and Matriculation Board (JAMB) has officially announced a significant adjustment to the minimum cut-off marks for the 2026 Unified Tertiary Matriculation Examination (UTME). In a major policy shift aimed at elevating academic standards, the new benchmark for university admissions has been raised across the board.

Following a consultative policy meeting with vice-chancellors, rectors, and provosts of tertiary institutions in Abuja, the board ratified the new baseline. For candidates targeting federal and state universities, the minimum acceptable score has jumped from 140 to 160.

Why The Sudden Increase?

According to the JAMB Registrar, the decision was not made arbitrarily. Data from the previous three years showed a concerning trend: candidates admitted with lower scores struggled significantly in their first year of study, leading to higher dropout rates in STEM and professional courses.

"We are not trying to deny anyone admission," the Registrar clarified during the press briefing. "We are simply ensuring that those who enter the university system are adequately prepared for the rigorous academic demands ahead. It's about quality assurance, not exclusion."

"We are simply ensuring that those who enter the university system are adequately prepared for the rigorous academic demands ahead."

What This Means for 2026 Candidates

If you are preparing for the 2026 UTME, the margin for error has effectively shrunk. Here is a breakdown of what the new policy entails:

  • Federal Universities: Minimum score of 160 (Note: Highly competitive courses like Medicine and Law will still require 250+).
  • Polytechnics: The benchmark has been raised from 100 to 120.
  • Colleges of Education: Maintained at 100 to encourage enrollment in the teaching profession.

Expert Advice: How to Adapt

Education consultants recommend a proactive approach. Dr. Emmanuel Olatunji, a senior academic advisor, suggests that candidates should pivot away from rote memorization and focus on deep conceptual understanding.

"The exam structure is also evolving. JAMB is integrating more critical thinking questions into subjects like Use of English and Physics," Dr. Olatunji noted. "Students must utilize modern tools. Taking simulated CBT tests is no longer optional—it is a necessity to build speed and accuracy."

With the exams just a few months away, the countdown has officially begun. The raised benchmark serves as a wake-up call: the era of minimal effort yielding university admission is effectively over. It is time to hit the books harder and smarter.
